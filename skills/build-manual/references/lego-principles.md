# LEGO Instruction Principles — the full treatment

LEGO manuals are a gold standard of wordless, universally-followable instructions. This reference explains why each principle works so you can apply it to any build, not just the examples here. Use it when planning the step sequence and planning and producing each panel image.

## Adaptation for real-photo manuals

This skill builds with real photographs wherever possible, which supersedes LEGO's vector-only rule (see SKILL.md, Image sourcing policy). How the principles map when panels come from photos:

- **#2 Highlight what's new** — photos can't be re-saturated, so highlighting becomes an overlay: a bold arrow or circle marking the part or spot that changes this step. On generated panels, keep the true LEGO treatment (full-saturation new parts against a muted existing build).
- **#3 Constant viewpoint** — aspirational with found photos; prefer shots matching the manual's dominant angle and call out orientation changes explicitly.
- **#4 Show, don't tell** — captions may be short sentences where a photo alone can't disambiguate (exact fuse slot, connector orientation).
- **#9 No hands** — a selection criterion, not a hard rule: reject cluttered hand-heavy shots when a cleaner one exists.
- **Found-photo honesty** — sourced photos may show a different model/variant than the parts in the build; when so, add a small corner note ("may differ from your parts"). Keep step numbers off the images — numbering is panel furniture, not image content.

When generation *is* used for a step, mimic illustration conventions — consistent angle, uncluttered background, clear new-vs-existing separation — using the sourced real photos as reference.

## Why LEGO uses illustrations, not photos

LEGO draws every step as a clean 3D illustration rather than photographing real bricks. Illustrations let you rotate, explode, and highlight parts freely, and they remove the shadows, glare, and texture that make photos hard to parse. The same virtual parts get reused across thousands of sets — build once, reuse everywhere.

This section still matters — it explains why generated step imagery should mimic clean illustration conventions (uncluttered, consistent angle, highlightable) rather than photorealistic clutter, and why part symbols/crops should be reused across panels for consistency.

## The principles, and the reasoning behind each

### 1. One clear action per step

Cognitive load is the enemy. A reader mid-build is already holding a lot in their head; each panel should ask them to do exactly one thing. If a step adds three unrelated parts, split it. Many small panels are cheaper for the reader than a few dense ones — this is the core reason LEGO manuals can be hundreds of pages yet feel effortless.

### 2. Highlight what's new (the most important one)

The reader's constant question is "what do I add now?" LEGO answers it visually. The new part is drawn crisp and full-color; everything already built recedes into a muted tone. If you get only one thing right, get this right. Without it, every step becomes a spot-the-difference puzzle.

### 3. Constant viewpoint

Keeping one isometric angle across the whole build means the reader never has to re-orient. Their mental model of the object stays stable step to step. When the model genuinely must be turned (to work on the back), show an explicit flip symbol between panels so the rotation is a deliberate, understood event rather than a confusing jump.

### 4. Show, don't tell

Pictures + a small fixed symbol set (arrows for motion, curved arrows for rotation, dashed lines for alignment, a click marker for joining, ×N for quantity) make the manual language-independent. Words appear only as numbers: step index, quantities, dimensions. This is what lets one manual ship worldwide.

### 5. Finished-result promise

Show the completed object first. Knowing the goal keeps the reader oriented and motivated — every step visibly moves toward that picture. Many LEGO pages also reprint a small finished-model thumbnail to anchor progress.

### 6. Sequence for early success and a stable base

Start from a solid foundation and build outward, adding a big satisfying chunk early. Physically this keeps the model stable as it grows; psychologically the early win builds momentum. Never require the reader to attach something to a part that isn't there yet — dependencies must be respected in the ordering.

### 7. Per-step parts call-out

Before a step, LEGO shows the exact bricks and counts it needs (a little parts box). The reader gathers those pieces, then performs the step. Replicate this with a parts-needed tag on each panel (part icon ×N) and a full parts inventory at the front of the manual.

### 8. Sub-assemblies as callouts

When a small component needs its own few steps, LEGO builds it in a boxed callout (often tinted), then shows it dropping into the main model as a single finished unit with a pointer arrow. This keeps the main sequence clean while still showing the detail. Nest callouts only shallowly — too many layers lose the reader.

### 9. No hands, no human figures

LEGO panels show only the model and the acting part — never hands placing bricks. Hands occlude exactly the region the reader needs to see. Convey the action with an arrow along the path of travel and by highlighting the new part. (IKEA uses a mascot figure; LEGO does not, and neither do we.)

### 10. Error prevention

Where a step is easy to get wrong (a part that fits two ways, a piece that must face a certain direction), pair a correct panel with a ✗ wrong one. Preventing the mistake on the page is far cheaper than the reader discovering it ten steps later.

## Applying LEGO grammar to non-LEGO tasks

The same grammar generalizes beyond brick models:

- **Furniture/devices**: parts → components; highlight the new panel/screw; callouts for drawer or hinge sub-assemblies.
- **Processes (recipes, setup, origami)**: parts → actions or states. Each panel shows the object's state after one action, with the change highlighted (a fold line, a poured ingredient, a toggled setting) and an arrow showing the motion.
- **Software/physical hybrids**: a "part" can be a state change; use zoom insets (detail views) to clarify a small control or connector.

The test is always the same: can someone follow it with the sound off — no words, just the pictures — and never wonder what to do next?

## Manual assembly order (what the finished booklet contains)

1. **Cover / finished-result panel** — the promise.
2. **Parts inventory** — every component with its quantity.
3. **Numbered step panels** — one action each, new part highlighted, parts-needed tag, arrows for motion, callouts and ✗ where needed.
4. **(Optional)** a repeated finished thumbnail on step pages for orientation.

Keep panel size, viewpoint, palette, and badge placement identical throughout — continuity is what makes independently rendered panels read as one manual.
